# Style brief: savanna

Status: **proposed** (assistant, 2026-10-25; John asked for savanna as its
own style, 2026-10-25; revised after the architect review of 2026-10-25).
Style file: none yet (ROADMAP 7.6); savanna uses
`desert.json` by fallback today (`biome-fallback.json`). Biomes: savanna,
savanna plateau, windswept savanna.

## Precedents

African savanna and Sahel building: Sahelian mud-brick towns (Djenné,
Timbuktu) with buttressed walls and wooden beam ends sticking out; Hausa
compounds (Kano, Zaria) with flat earth roofs and relief-decorated walls;
round and square huts with steep thatch roofs across East and Southern
Africa; Ndebele houses with painted geometric bands; the vanilla savanna
village (acacia and dyed terracotta). Compounds of several small buildings
round a swept yard, shade first, low walls of earth.

## Materials

Walls of packed mud and mud bricks, or terracotta in earth tones (plain,
orange, brown). White terracotta is a light greyish tan, not white: use it
as a pale earth render, and calcite for a true lime-washed white band, with
one coloured band at the base or eaves (Ndebele-style). Acacia logs as
posts, beams and projecting beam ends (fine at house scale; DS-1 bars them
over 6 storeys), acacia planks for doors, shutters and verandas, acacia
fences round yards. Roofs of hay bales (thatch) or acacia; a mud-brick or
red sandstone plinth. Hay and packed mud have no stairs or slabs: parapets
and copings in mud bricks (stairs, slabs and walls exist), and see Roof for
thatch. Town and city: terracotta, mud bricks, red sandstone, granite trim;
tall buildings in terracotta and brick (DS-1). Savanna is an open biome:
fewer wood-walled buildings than in forests (DS-29). Interiors acacia and
stripped acacia, terracotta floors, orange, yellow, brown and red cloth,
hay, potted dead bushes or acacia saplings.

## Proportions and massing

1-2 storeys, small footprints, thick walls, a shaded veranda on posts.
Later (needs lot layout, not a style file): compounds of 2-4 small blocks
round a yard rather than one large house, and round plans for huts. For
7.6, judge a single building on its lot.

## Roof

Two families, chosen per settlement: steep thatch (hip, 1:1 or steeper,
1-block overhang; conical on round huts later) in villages and hamlets;
flat earth roofs with a low parapet in towns and cities (the Sahel look).
A hay roof is stepped full blocks (no hay stairs or slabs): accept the 1:1
stepped thatch as the look, or finish the eaves and ridge with acacia
stairs. No gables on stone or brick buildings.

**Sahel town signature** (what separates it from desert, which stays smooth
and blank): mud-brick pilasters or buttresses at regular spacing up the
walls, rising above the parapet as pinnacles (Djenné); a mud-brick parapet;
acacia beam ends projecting at every floor line, not only below the roof.

## Windows and doors

Small openings, few on the street side; wooden shutters (trapdoors) rather
than much glass; doors under a deep lintel or the veranda. A separate
kitchen building in the compound is later (lot layout).

## Avoid

The pale sandstone look (that is desert); a flat-roofed blank box with beam
ends only at the roof (also desert); cool grey stone walls; large glass;
pine-forest woods (spruce, dark oak) as walls; mixing several terracotta
colours on one face (one wall colour plus one band, DS-2); bright concrete;
promising white walls from white terracotta.
